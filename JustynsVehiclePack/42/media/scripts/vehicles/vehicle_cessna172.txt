/*
    Cessna 172 - flyable single-engine light aircraft.

    ================================ MODEL PROVENANCE ================================
    3D model: "Plane" by osmosikum
    https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/plane-cedc8a07370747f7b0d14400cdf2faf9
    Licensed CC-BY-4.0 (http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)
    The licence is embedded in the source .glb's asset.extras block. See CREDITS.txt at
    the mod root for the full attribution.

    ================================ IMPORT NOTES ====================================
    Converted by tools/convert_plane_model.py, which is reproducible and prints every
    number in this file. Nothing below was eyeballed.

    NODE CHAIN BAKED. The source's transforms live entirely on its node chain, and that
    chain is a real ~15 degree yaw plus a tilt - not an axis permutation. It is resolved
    to world space and baked into the vertices before export, so every
    FrameTransformMatrix in Vehicles_Cessna172.x is identity and nothing relies on PZ's
    importer to orient anything. (AircraftMod's Hind import produced a squashed
    4.67-unit-tall helicopter precisely by leaving that to the importer.)

    AXES MEASURED, NOT GUESSED. The lateral axis is the thin principal axis of the
    VERTICAL stabiliser; the up axis is the thin principal axis of the HORIZONTAL
    stabiliser; forward is their cross product, sign-checked against the propeller's
    centroid. The frame is re-orthonormalised so det == +1 - a proper rotation, so
    winding order and normals stay valid and no faces needed flipping. PZ convention:
    +Z nose, +Y up, +X lateral (BaseVehicle.getForwardVector() is local Z).

    SCALE. The source is 115.49 units per metre (its 1270.4-unit wingspan against a real
    172's 11.0 m span). Scaled so the aircraft renders at 0.5804 script units per real
    metre - which is the MiG-29 mod's OWN rendered density (that mod draws a 17.3 m
    airframe at 10.04 units). Deliberate: the two aircraft then sit correctly next to
    each other in one world. It also lands the numbers where they should be - this
    Cessna's 6.38-unit wingspan against the MiG's 6.46 mirrors the real 11.0 m vs
    11.36 m, and its 4.59-unit length against the MiG's 10.04 mirrors 8.28 m vs 17.3 m.
    Resulting airframe: length Z 4.588, wingspan X 6.384, height Y 1.748.
    To make the aircraft larger or smaller, change `scale` in BOTH model blocks below
    AND multiply every spatial figure in this file by the same factor - see the MiG's
    own scale note for which fields are spatial and which are physics response curves.

    ORIGIN IS AT THE CENTRE OF GRAVITY, NOT THE BOUNDING-BOX CENTRE. A Cessna has a long
    tail, so the geometric middle sits well behind the CG - and with the origin there,
    BOTH the nose gear and the main gear end up forward of it, which makes a tricycle
    layout tip back onto its tail the moment physics starts. The converter places the
    origin 25% of the wheelbase ahead of the main wheels instead, where a real 172's CG
    is. Main gear then sits BEHIND the centre of mass and the nose gear ahead of it,
    which is what keeps the aircraft sitting on its nosewheel.

    WHEELS are on the model's ACTUAL measured contact patches (the converter finds the
    three ground-contact clusters and reports them), not guessed:
        nose gear   X  0.000  Z +0.707  contact Y -0.874
        main gear   X +-0.703 Z -0.234  contact Y -0.803
    A 172 has THREE wheels; PZ needs four, so the two "front" wheels sit close together
    on the nose-gear centreline - the same thing the MiG-29 does for its single nose
    wheel. offset Y = contact + radius, so each tyre rests exactly on the ground the
    gear is drawn touching. The nose contact is 0.071 lower than the mains because the
    model is posed with the nose strut extended; carrying that difference through into
    the offsets is what makes the aircraft's physics attitude match its drawn attitude
    instead of fighting it.

    FIXED GEAR - no retraction, and no gearUp/gearDown model pair. A 172's gear does not
    retract, so unlike the MiG there is nothing for Lua to toggle and no hide/show cycle
    that could produce an in-between frame.

    VANILLA TIRE MODELS ARE SUPPRESSED (C172Flight.lua hides them permanently for this
    vehicle only). The airframe mesh draws its own spatted wheels and gear legs, so
    leaving vanilla's car tyres on would put a second set of black car wheels through
    the aircraft's own. The Tire PARTS still exist and still work normally - they are
    only made invisible.

    GROUND HANDLING is left to vanilla, deliberately, exactly as the MiG does it: the
    aircraft genuinely wants wheel-driven physics for taxi and the takeoff roll, and
    that is how "rolls like an aeroplane on its gear" is achieved without writing a
    ground controller. engineForce, maxSpeed and steeringClamp below are what make it
    feel like a light aircraft rather than a car - see each field's own note.
*/
module Base
{
	/* 1.10.0 SHADER Cessna172_vehicle (42/media/shaders, built by tools/build_shaders.py): the game's own
	   vehicle_noreflect plus two things, so the people inside can be seen - Jay: "i can see myself in
	   mig now can you do the same for cessna". The window texels (alpha 128, tools/build_glass.py) draw
	   as see-through glass, and the whole aircraft can be moved in depth to agree with its occupants
	   (client/Cessna172/C172PilotView.lua). BOTH model blocks use it - the propeller must move with the
	   nose it is mounted on. With no occupant it draws exactly like vehicle_noreflect. */
	model Vehicles_Cessna172
	{
		mesh = vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172|Cessna172_Body,
		shader = Cessna172_vehicle,
		invertX = false,
		scale = 1.75,
	}

	/* The propeller is its own mesh so C172PropSpin.lua can rotate it (1.9.0; it was
	   Patch_PropSpin.java). There is exactly ONE of these - no pre-rotated pose set -
	   because animating blades by swapping models needs a hide and a show per step and the
	   renderer can catch the frame between them, which is what produces a
	   ghost/duplicate propeller. */
	model Cessna172_Prop
	{
		mesh = vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172|Cessna172_Prop,
		shader = Cessna172_vehicle,
		invertX = false,
		scale = 1.75,
	}

	/* MODEL SCALE 2.0 -> 1.75 (2026-08-31, at Jay's request: "a little smaller").
	   This is a ONE-VALUE change by design. VehicleScript.Loaded() multiplies every spatial
	   field in this file by the model block's scale on load - extents, physicsChassisShape,
	   centerOfMassOffset, every wheel offset and radius, suspensionRestLength,
	   maxSuspensionTravelCm, all interaction areas and all model offsets - so the collider,
	   the wheels, the suspension and the hotspots all shrink with the mesh automatically and
	   stay consistent with each other.
	   PASSENGER position offsets are scaled too: Loaded() multiplies every passenger position's
	   offset by the model scale (42.21 bytecode, VehicleScript.Loaded 497-523), so a seat offset
	   below is in the same units as everything else here and lands x1.75 at runtime. (An older note
	   here said they had to be multiplied by 0.875 by hand; the bytecode says otherwise.)
	   ONE THING DOES NOT FOLLOW AUTOMATICALLY and was handled by hand:
	     * Anything in Lua/Java compared against a WORLD quantity. The only one was the
	       wheels-on-ground rest length, which now reads the real scaled value back from the
	       script (c172GetRestLength) instead of carrying its own copy.
	   The propeller hub pivot in C172PropSpin.lua (raw mesh units, times the 1.75 mesh
	   scale) needs no change for this either. */
	vehicle Cessna172
	{
		mechanicType = 1,
		engineRepairLevel = 3,

		/* Same real, vanilla-supported per-vehicle multiplier on occupant crash injury
		   the MiG uses (BaseVehicle.addRandomDamageFromCrash multiplies realDamage by
		   it). Stacks with Patch_LandingDurability.java's softening of the crash
		   severity number itself as a second, pilot-specific layer. 0.45, not
		   near-zero: a genuinely bad arrival must still hurt. */
		playerDamageProtection = 0.45,

		model
		{
			file = Vehicles_Cessna172,
			scale = 1.7500,
			offset = 0.0000 0.0000 0.0000,
		}

		skin
		{
			texture = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Shell,
			/* GROUND SHADOW. PZ draws a vehicle's shadow from a texture -
			   BaseVehicle.renderShadow() calls getShadowTexture(), which resolves through
			   VehicleScript.getTextures() to this field. With nothing declared the aircraft
			   fell back to a plain vehicle-sized box, which is what a 6.38-unit wingspan
			   aeroplane casting a rectangle looked like.

			   The texture is PROJECTED FROM THE MODEL by tools/build_shadow_texture.py -
			   the airframe mesh flattened to its plan view - so it cannot drift out of step
			   with the aircraft, and re-running that script after any model change
			   regenerates it. The engine rotates the texture with the vehicle, so the
			   shadow turns with the aeroplane without anything tracking heading at runtime.

			   Altitude behaviour is unchanged and still lives in Patch_AirborneShadow,
			   which fades the alpha and shrinks the extents with height. */
			textureShadow = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Shadow,
		}

		/* ================= ALL VANILLA CAR AUDIO IS CLOSED OFF HERE =================

		   THE PROBLEM THIS SOLVES. B42 auto-generates a default AUTOMOBILE VehicleSounds
		   object for every BaseVehicle regardless of what a script declares, and every one
		   of its sound-name getters FALLS BACK to a vanilla car event when the script leaves
		   that slot empty. Read straight out of VehicleSounds' bytecode - each getter tests
		   `getScript().getSounds().<field> != null` and otherwise returns a literal:

		       getEngineSound()              -> "VehicleEngineDefault"
		       getEngineStartSound()         -> "VehicleStarted"
		       getEngineTurnOffSound()       -> "VehicleTurnedOff"
		       getHandBrakeSound()           -> "VehicleHandBrake"
		       getIgnitionFailSound()        -> "VehicleFailingToStart"
		       getIgnitionFailNoPowerSound() -> SoundKey.VEHICLE_IGNITION_FAIL_DEFAULT

		   Declaring NO sound block therefore did not mean "no sound" - it meant "all six
		   vanilla car sounds", which is exactly the automobile engine audio that was still
		   audible underneath the Cessna's own loops. Naming a Cessna sound in every slot is
		   what actually closes it, and it closes it for THIS VEHICLE ONLY: no other vehicle,
		   vanilla or modded, reads this file.

		   WHY THE SCRIPT AND NOT ONLY THE JAVA PATCH. Patch_NoAirborneVehicleSound's
		   isEngineSounding override (now Cessna-wide rather than airborne-only) silences the
		   LOOP on the local client. It cannot reach two other things:
		     * the ONE-SHOTS - start, turn-off, handbrake, ignition-fail - which are played
		       directly by BaseVehicle and never consult isEngineSounding at all;
		     * zombie.vehicleNetworkSound.client.VehicleState, the MP relay that plays a
		       distant vehicle's engine when it is outside this client's simulation range.
		       That class has its OWN isEngineSounding and its own VehicleSounds, but it
		       resolves sound NAMES through the same VehicleScript - so a named slot here is
		       the only thing that covers it.
		   Both layers are deliberate: the script closes the fallback everywhere, the patch
		   additionally stops a voice ever being opened locally.

		   engine / engineStart / engineTurnOff / handBrake -> the silent stub, because
		   C172Flight.lua's updateEngineAudio already owns the running loops and the start and
		   shutdown one-shots, and it drives them for every Cessna in range (including other
		   players'). Letting vanilla play its versions too would double them.

		   ignitionFail / ignitionFailNoPower -> real Cessna clips, LEFT to vanilla on
		   purpose: BaseVehicle already fires these at exactly the right moment in its
		   ignition path, that path is not something this mod simulates, and the sounds are
		   the aircraft's own starter, so nothing car-like is heard.

		   The three-layer running engine bed is NOT expressible here - a piston aeroplane
		   needs an idle bed and a power layer crossfaded against RPM simultaneously and a
		   single engine= slot cannot say that. See cessna172_sounds.txt. */
		sound
		{
			engine = C172Silent,
			engineStart = C172Silent,
			engineTurnOff = C172Silent,
			handBrake = C172Silent,
			ignitionFail = C172StarterFail,
			ignitionFailNoPower = C172NoPower,
		}

		textureMask = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Mask,
		textureLights = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Lights,
		textureDamage1Overlay = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Overlay1,
		textureDamage2Overlay = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Overlay2,
		textureDamage1Shell = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Damage1,
		textureDamage2Shell = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Damage2,
		textureRust = Vehicles/Vehicles_Cessna172_Rust,

		/* COLLIDER. `extents` and `physicsChassisShape` are HALF-extents, not full sizes -
		   verified against vanilla rather than assumed: the van declares 0.9341 0.7253 2.3297
		   and a PZ van is about 1.9 m wide and 4.7 m long, so each figure is half the real
		   dimension. (BaseVehicle.getExtents() returns halfWidth, halfHeight, halfLength.)
		   Getting this wrong doubles the collision box silently.

		   0.85 x 0.70 x 1.50 is a 1.70 x 1.40 x 3.00 box: roughly the fuselage and cabin, on
		   a 6.38-wide, 1.75-tall, 4.59-long aeroplane. The wings and the tail deliberately
		   overhang it - a full-span box would snag the aircraft on every fence, tree and
		   parked car it visually clears while taxiing, and it is also what "no giant
		   invisible collision boxes" means in practice. The box bottom sits at -0.70 while
		   the tyres contact at -0.87, which is correct for a raycast vehicle: the wheels hang
		   below the chassis shape and carry it on their suspension rays. */
		extents = 0.8500 0.7000 1.5000,
		physicsChassisShape = 0.8500 0.7000 1.5000,

		/* Real 172 figures: 767 kg empty, 1111 kg max. Heavier than every vanilla car
		   (800-1030) which is correct, and it is what the flight model's lift controller is
		   sized against (see LIFT_MASS / LIFT_MAX_FORCE in C172Flight.lua). */
		mass = 1000,

		/* CENTRE OF MASS - MOVED FORWARD AND DOWN IN 0.1.4 TO STOP THE AIRCRAFT SITTING
		   DOWN ON ITS TAIL THE MOMENT THROTTLE IS APPLIED.

		   THE BUG THIS FIXES. Pressing W buried the tail in the ground and wedged the
		   aircraft. It is not a collision or a terrain problem, it is a pitch-balance
		   problem, and the arithmetic is not close:

		   PZ does not push an aircraft with its propeller. It drives it through the WHEEL
		   CONTACT PATCHES, which sit ~0.83 below the centre of mass, so every newton of
		   drive force also applies a nose-UP pitching torque about the main-gear axle.
		   The only thing resisting that is the weight acting on the arm between the centre
		   of mass and that axle. With the CoM at Z 0.0 and the mains at Z -0.2343 that arm
		   was 0.234 - and the numbers came out:

		       tipping  = 1800 N x 0.827 =  1488 Nm
		       restoring= 9810 N x 0.234 =  2298 Nm      margin 1.54x

		   1.54x is not a margin, it is a coin toss: any transient - a bump, a gear change,
		   an RPM spike - exceeds it. The MiG, which has never done this, runs 4.41x, and
		   the reason is entirely geometric: its CoM sits 1.244 ahead of its mains, over
		   five times this aircraft's arm.

		   And when it does rotate, it rotates FAR. The tail tip is at Z -3.270 (measured
		   from the mesh, not estimated), which is 3.50 units behind the main gear - 3.7x
		   the 0.941 wheelbase. Worse, the nose gear runs out of suspension travel and goes
		   completely slack at only 7.3 degrees of pitch, at which point there is no
		   restoring moment left at all and the tail has already dropped 0.44 units. There
		   is no collider out there to catch it - `extents` reaches nowhere near the tail,
		   deliberately - so it passes straight through the terrain and jams.

		   THE FIX, and why it is these two numbers.
		   Z +0.40 moves the CoM ahead of the mains, lengthening the restoring arm from
		   0.234 to 0.634. Y -0.25 (from -0.10) lowers it, shortening the tipping arm from
		   0.827 to 0.677. Together:

		       tipping  = 1800 N x 0.677 =  1218 Nm
		       restoring= 9810 N x 0.634 =  6222 Nm      margin 5.11x

		   which is comfortably past the MiG's proven 4.41x. Static nose-gear load becomes
		   67% - heavy for a real 172, but the MiG runs 63% and is stable, and rotation for
		   takeoff is done by the flight model at 55 km/h, not by the drivetrain, so a
		   nose-heavy ground stance costs nothing.

		   WHY NOT JUST MOVE THE WHEELS AFT: the wheel offsets are the model's own measured
		   contact patches, so moving them would put the physics gear somewhere the aircraft
		   is not drawn touching. centerOfMassOffset is a pure physics parameter - it does
		   not move the mesh.

		   SPAWN HEIGHT IS UNCHANGED, verified rather than assumed. CarController's
		   constructor spawns at -min(modelOffset.y + wheel(0).offset.y - wheel(0).radius,
		   centerOfMassOffset.y - extents.y / 2) = -min(-0.8742, -0.60), and the nose-gear
		   term still wins exactly as before.

		   Y -0.25 still sits 0.68 above the contact patches, so the inverted-pendulum
		   failure the old comment warned about is still nowhere near - that needs the CoM
		   at or below the contacts. Note also that PZ's own Java centres the vehicle's
		   collision footprint on this offset (xMin = com.x - ext.x/2, zMin = com.z -
		   ext.z/2, in isPositionOnLeftOrRight and getClosestPointOnEdge), so the Z +0.40
		   shifts that footprint forward by 0.40 as well. On a box only 1.5 long under a
		   4.6-long airframe that is a minor change, and it moves the footprint toward
		   where the mass actually is. */
		centerOfMassOffset = 0.0000 -0.1000 -0.3000,

		/* LIGHT AIRCRAFT GROUND CHARACTER, calibrated against vanilla rather than guessed.
		   Vanilla force-to-mass ratios run from 3.88 (loaded pickup) to 8.75 (racecar); the
		   MiG is 3.38. At 3000/1000 this is 3.0 - deliberately the slowest-accelerating
		   thing in the game, so the takeoff roll is a long, unhurried build rather than a
		   shove, which is the single biggest reason this does not feel like a slower jet.
		   It is still comfortably above the point where rolling resistance would stall it:
		   an earlier 1250 would have been a third of the weakest vanilla vehicle and might
		   simply never have reached rotation speed.

		   maxSpeed 120 is the vanilla DRIVETRAIN's ceiling, used on the ground only. Airborne
		   speed is C172Flight.lua's own MAX_AIRSPEED_KMH (118) and is not read from here. */
		engineForce = 3000,
		maxSpeed = 120f,
		engineLoudness = 55,
		engineQuality = 70,

		/* Small disc brakes, but strong enough to actually stop a landing roll. */
		/* Raised with the steering (was 65) so the aircraft can actually be pulled up during
		   the rollout instead of coasting the length of the runway. */
		brakingForce = 90,

		/* Four gears with an even ladder. Vanilla only ever uses 4 or 5, so this stays on the
		   proven path rather than trying 1 or 2 to imitate a fixed-pitch prop's direct drive;
		   evenly spaced ratios keep each upshift's torque dip small, which is what "smooth
		   gradual takeoff" needs from the ground half of the roll. The engine AUDIO does not
		   read the gearbox at all - it is driven from airspeed - so shifts are never audible. */
		gearRatioCount = 4,
		gearRatioR = 3.2,
		gearRatio1 = 2.6,
		gearRatio2 = 1.9,
		gearRatio3 = 1.4,
		gearRatio4 = 1.0,

		extentsOffset = 0.5 0.5,

		/* Coasts freely - an aeroplane on its gear has far less rolling drag than a car
		   (vanilla van 5.0, sports 2.0). */
		stoppingMovementForce = 2.5f,

		/* Vanilla cars use 0.7. Almost none here: the aircraft must stay flat on its gear
		   through a turn rather than leaning onto a wingtip. */
		rollInfluence = 0.08f,

		/* Nosewheel steering, not car steering: slower to apply and limited in authority,
		   which is what stops the aircraft darting sideways during the takeoff roll. Vanilla
		   cars are 0.03/0.30; this is well under half that, but not so low that it cannot be
		   taxied around a corner. */
		/* GROUND STEERING, raised 2026-08-31 (was 0.018 / 0.13). Those figures were
		   inherited from the MiG-29, where a deliberately heavy, un-darty jet was the goal.
		   They are wrong for this aircraft: a Cessna has a steerable nosewheel and is nimble
		   on the ground, and at the inherited values the landing rollout and taxi were very
		   hard to point - which is the "I lose steering authority when I touch down" problem.
		   These are now nearer a normal vanilla car's responsiveness. */
		steeringIncrement = 0.045,
		steeringClamp = 0.34,

		/* Gear spring rate. RAISED TO THE MiG'S PROVEN TRIO IN 0.1.6 (48 / 4.5 / 3.6) as
		   the third part of the taxi-wobble fix.

		   The old 32 was chosen to be soft so a touchdown would settle rather than bounce.
		   Soft is the wrong answer here: at 32 the aircraft's own weight used most of the
		   available travel before it had hit anything, leaving the nose gear 0.0167 from
		   its bump stop (see the MAIN GEAR note below). 48 compresses about a third less
		   for the same load, which is what buys the headroom back.

		   Compression and damping scale with sqrt(stiffness) in PZ's own numbers - the
		   vanilla van is 30 / 3.83 / 2.88 and the MiG 48 / 4.5 / 3.6, both landing on the
		   same damping ratios - so 4.5 / 3.6 is what 48 is supposed to be paired with, not
		   a guess. The MiG lands on these without bouncing.

		   maxSuspensionTravelCm stays 12 (the MiG uses 9): the travel is not the problem,
		   the aircraft using all of it while parked was, and the extra headroom is worth
		   keeping now that it is genuinely spare.

		   suspensionRestLength MUST match WHEEL_REST_LENGTH in C172Flight.lua. */
		suspensionStiffness = 48,
		suspensionCompression = 4.5,
		suspensionDamping = 3.6,
		maxSuspensionTravelCm = 12,
		suspensionRestLength = 0.20f,
		wheelFriction = 1.5f,
		frontEndHealth = 100,
		rearEndHealth = 100,
		seats = 2,

		/* NOSE GEAR - PHYSICS ONLY, AND THE X SPACING IS DELIBERATELY NOT THE MODEL'S.

		   X WIDENED IN 0.1.8 from +-0.0900 to +-0.7029. THIS IS THE FIX FOR THE AIRCRAFT
		   ROLLING ONTO ITS BACK WHILE TAXIING, and it is worth the arithmetic because the
		   old value looked completely reasonable and was catastrophic.

		   A real 172 has three wheels; PZ needs four; so the two "front" wheels were put
		   close together on the nose-gear centreline to imitate one. That is correct as a
		   DRAWING and disastrous as a SUPPORT POLYGON. A vehicle tips about the line
		   joining the front and rear wheel on the side it is leaning toward - and with the
		   front pair on the centreline, that line runs steeply inboard-diagonal instead of
		   straight down the side, so the centre of mass sits barely any distance from it.

		   Measured as the perpendicular distance from the CoM to that tip-over line,
		   divided by the CoM's height above the contact plane (a vanilla car is ~1.0-1.2 g):

		       nose +-0.0900, CoM z 0.40  (the build that flipped)   0.263 g
		       nose +-0.0900, CoM z 0.30  (0.1.7)                    0.322 g
		       nose +-0.4500                                         0.585 g
		       nose +-0.7029  (this)                                 0.761 g
		       MiG-29, stable in game, for reference                 0.357 g

		   0.263 g is less lateral acceleration than a gentle turn at taxi speed. The
		   aircraft was not being tipped by anything unusual; it was tipping because four
		   wheels arranged in a triangle have almost no roll base. Once over, the wings and
		   tail intersect the terrain - and nothing out there has a collider - so it wedges,
		   which is the "stuck in the ground" half of the same report.

		   WHY THIS COSTS NOTHING VISUALLY: the vanilla tire models are permanently hidden
		   for this vehicle (see hideVanillaWheels in C172Flight.lua) and the airframe mesh
		   draws its own gear, which does not move with these offsets. So this changes the
		   physics support polygon and NOTHING ELSE - the aircraft still looks like it has
		   one nose wheel on the centreline. Matching the main gear's +-0.7029 makes the
		   support a proper rectangle.

		   Y and Z are untouched: Y because wheel(0) is what vanilla's spawn formula
		   measures the aircraft from and moving it walks the collider toward the ground
		   (see the 0.1.4 regression note in HANDOFF.md), Z because it sets the wheelbase
		   and the nose/main load split, which are already solved.

		   offset Y = measured contact (-0.8742) + radius (0.11). */
		wheel FrontLeft
		{
			front = true,
			offset = 0.7029 -0.7642 0.7065,
			radius = 0.11f,
			width = 0.13f,
		}

		wheel FrontRight
		{
			front = true,
			offset = -0.7029 -0.7642 0.7065,
			radius = 0.11f,
			width = 0.13f,
		}

		/* MAIN GEAR.

		   Z MOVED AFT IN 0.1.10, from -0.2343 to -1.8000. THIS IS THE ROOT-CAUSE FIX FOR
		   THE AIRCRAFT SITTING DOWN ON ITS TAIL, and it is the same trick as the nose
		   gear's X above: wheel positions are PHYSICS ONLY here, because the vanilla tire
		   models are permanently hidden for this vehicle and the airframe mesh draws its
		   own gear, which does not follow these offsets. Moving them changes the support
		   polygon and nothing on screen.

		   The problem was never the springs or the centre of mass - it was the lever. The
		   main gear sat at -0.2343 because that is where the model draws it, giving a
		   0.941 wheelbase underneath a tail that reaches Z -3.270. That is 3.23 units of
		   overhang behind the axle against 0.941 between the axles - a 3.2:1 lever, with
		   the drive force applied at the contact patches nearly a unit below the centre of
		   mass. Four builds of stiffness and CoM tuning could not make that safe, and a
		   real flight log caught it going from 9.8 to 89.8 degrees of pitch in one second.

		       mainZ    comZ    wheelbase  tail overhang  overhang/wb  tail-sit margin
		       -0.2343   0.30      0.941        3.036        3.23x         3.15x
		       -1.8000  -0.30      2.506        1.470        0.59x         8.86x
		       MiG-29, stable in game                                      4.41x

		   With the axle under the rear fuselage the overhang is shorter than the wheelbase
		   instead of triple it, and the aircraft simply has no leverage to rotate. The
		   centre of mass moves with it, to -0.30, which keeps a sane 60/40 nose/main load
		   split - leaving it at +0.30 would have put 84% on the nose.

		   ONLY Z MOVED. Y stays at the 0.1.6 value so both axles keep their shared contact
		   plane, and X stays at +-0.7029 so the support stays the rectangle the nose-gear
		   note above explains. The TireRearLeft/TireRearRight `area` hotspots are also
		   deliberately left at -0.2343: those are where the player CLICKS to service a
		   wheel, so they belong where the wheel is drawn, not where physics carries it.

		   Y LOWERED IN 0.1.6, from -0.6836 to -0.7542, so that BOTH AXLES SHARE ONE
		   UNLOADED CONTACT PLANE. This is the taxi-wobble fix and it is worth spelling out.

		   The original offsets used the convention `offset Y = measured contact + radius`,
		   which carried the model's real 0.0706 nose-low pose straight into the physics.
		   But a Bullet raycast wheel does not rest at `offset - radius` - it rests at
		   `offset - suspensionLength - radius`, and both axles share one restLength. So
		   that 0.0706 was not an attitude, it was 0.0706 of PERMANENT PRE-COMPRESSION on
		   the nose gear, applied before the aircraft carried any weight at all.

		   Stacked on top of a centre of mass that was putting 67% of the weight on that
		   same nose gear, the parked stance came out (solved, not estimated):

		       0.1.5:  L_nose = 0.0967   with the bump stop at 0.08   -> 0.0167 of travel left

		   Sixteen thousandths, parked, on flat tarmac, doing nothing. Any bump, any pitch,
		   any load transfer slammed the nose gear into its stop, the suspension went rigid,
		   and the aircraft jolted - which is what the taxi wobble was.

		   Both axles now share an unloaded contact plane at -1.0742. Combined with the
		   centre-of-mass and stiffness changes above, the parked stance becomes:

		       0.1.6:  L_nose = 0.1420, L_main = 0.1558   -> 0.062 / 0.076 of travel left

		   i.e. sitting in the middle of the suspension's range with room to move in both
		   directions, which is what "the wheels stay on the ground" actually requires.

		   WHY LOWER THE MAINS RATHER THAN RAISE THE NOSE: raising the nose would raise
		   wheel(0), and wheel(0) is what vanilla's spawn formula measures the aircraft
		   from. That would have lifted the aircraft 0.07 and left the collider's bottom
		   face only 0.0036 above the ground - which is how 0.1.4 fell through the map.
		   Lowering the mains leaves wheel(0) untouched, so the spawn height and the
		   collider clearance stay at the values already proven not to fall through.

		   The physics main gear now sits 0.0706 lower than the model draws it - 1.5% of the
		   airframe's length, and invisible in an isometric view. */
